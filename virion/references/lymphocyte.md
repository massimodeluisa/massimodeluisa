# Lymphocyte

One resting small lymphocyte from human blood. Not a virion, and not a T cell or a B cell: light microscopy and the electron microscope do not separate those two. The figure is the resting cell. Activation, blast change, and plasma-cell cytoplasm are a different object.

## What is drawn

The undeformed cell is a sphere. Schmid-Schönbein, Shih, and Chien measured it by transmission electron microscopy at 6.2 μm across, and by light microscopy at 5.75 μm. The same paper's isotonic volume, read from their figure by BioNumbers, is 125 ± 15 μm³, which is the sphere of that 6.2 μm diameter. Downey and colleagues later measured an optical diameter of 6.05 μm (donor means 5.98–6.09). A blood film flattens the cell, and the small lymphocyte on a film is given as 6–9 μm.

The nucleus is 44.4% of the cell volume in that stereology, the cytoplasm 51.6%, mitochondria 3.8%, and granules 0.2%. On a flattened film the nucleus can fill about 90% of the area. That is the film, not the sphere, so this drawing uses the volume fraction. The plasma membrane has about 130% more area than the smooth sphere, which is the folding and the few microvilli. The villus thickness on the card is a drawing weight. The sources do not give a villus count, and the readout does not invent one.

Janeway's Figure 1.5 is the resting cell: condensed chromatin, a thin rim of cytoplasm, and no rough endoplasmic reticulum worth drawing. The nucleolus is small and round in section; the drawing uses 0.9 μm, inside the 0.5–1.4 μm range reported for nucleoli in section. Heterochromatin sits in blocks against the nuclear envelope. A few mitochondrial profiles sit in the thicker side of the rim. The readout N is the nuclear share computed from the two radii in the model.

## Left out

No culture, no mitogen, no receptor sequence, and no surface-marker panel. T and B cells are not drawn as different shapes. The large granular lymphocyte, with its handful of granules, is a different cell.
